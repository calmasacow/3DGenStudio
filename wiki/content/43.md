# Kimodo

Describe a motion in plain English and get an animation for your rigged mesh. The **Kimodo** tab (in the Mesh Editor's Auto Rig side panel, next to **[Skeleton](33.md)** and **[Animations](34.md)**) sends your prompt to NVIDIA's **Kimodo** text-to-motion model, which generates a motion on its own skeleton; that motion is then retargeted onto your mesh through the same machinery the Animations tab uses.

The difference between the two tabs is only where the motion comes from. **Animations** picks a clip from a fixed library of pre-animated reference characters. **Kimodo** invents one from a sentence. Everything downstream — bone mapping, live preview, the clip grid, saving the mesh with animations embedded — is shared.

> Kimodo runs on the **motion service**, configured in **Settings → Mesh Tools → Motion Generation**. It needs an **NVIDIA GPU**. The first run downloads roughly **17 GB** of model weights, so allow time for it. In the desktop app the service is installed from Settings and started for you.

> **Built with Meta Llama 3.** Kimodo encodes your prompt with LLM2Vec, which is built on Meta Llama 3, so installing it downloads Meta Llama 3-8B-Instruct (~16 GB of that 17 GB). Those weights are licensed under the **Meta Llama 3 Community License**, which you have to accept before the install runs — the installer and Settings both show the full text. Nothing else in the app depends on it: leave Kimodo uninstalled and Auto Rig and the **[Animations](34.md)** library work as normal.

> This tab only works on a **rigged** mesh. Run **[Auto Rig](32.md)** first, or load a mesh that already has a skeleton.

## Workflow

1. Click **Map bones** to match Kimodo's skeleton to your mesh (see **[Bone Mapping](35.md)**). You can do this before generating anything — the service hands over its skeleton without loading the model, so there is no wait.
2. Type a prompt under **Describe the motion**.
3. Set **Duration per sentence (seconds)**.
4. Click **Generate motion** and wait. A progress bar reports each stage.
5. The finished clip lands in the **Animations** grid at the bottom of the tab and starts playing on your mesh.
6. **Tick** the clips you want and click **Save mesh with N animations** to embed them and save the mesh as a new version.

If you skip step 1, the bones are mapped **automatically** on the first generation and the panel says so — check the mapping if the motion comes out wrong.

## Writing a prompt

Start with *"A person…"* and keep it to one or two actions. Kimodo knows locomotion, gestures, everyday activities, combat, dance, and styles such as tired, drunk or sneaky.

```
A person walks forward and waves.
A person jumps very high.
A person runs fast, then stops and looks around.
```

**Ten seconds per sentence is the model's ceiling**, not ours. To get past it, write several sentences: each one is generated in turn and the results are blended together. The panel adds up what you have written — *"3 sentences × 5s = 15.0s total"* — so you can see the length before committing to the GPU time. Sentences are split on full stops.

## Bone mapping

Kimodo's skeleton occupies the **same single source-rig slot** as a reference mesh from the Animations tab. Mapping Kimodo's bones therefore *replaces* whatever the Animations tab had mapped, and vice versa. Both tabs tell you when the other one is holding the slot.

Kimodo drives 23 body bones. It animates **no fingers at all** — it solves a compact skeleton and fills every knuckle from one fixed relaxed pose — which is why the fingers are left off the mapping and handled separately by Hand curl, below.

## Playback options

Once a mapping exists, these appear above the clip grid.

| Control | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Match reference rest pose** | Pose your mesh like Kimodo's skeleton before the motion is applied. On by default. Turn it off to keep your mesh's own stance — a character modelled with its legs apart will then keep them apart for the whole clip. |
| **Convert to in-place** | Strip the forward/sideways travel so the character animates on the spot. Jumps, crouches and turns are kept. See below. |
| **Hand curl (open → fist)** | Kimodo never moves fingers, so this holds them in a chosen pose for the whole clip: a slider per hand, plus one per thumb. **Axis** picks which local axis the thumb folds about — which one a rig uses is a convention, not something that can be read off the shape, so if a thumb folds the wrong way, try another axis or the flip button. |

### Convert to in-place

This is a **post-process on playback**, not a property of the motion. The stored motion always travels; ticking the box strips the travel on the way onto your mesh. So you can turn it on and off freely, and it applies to motions generated long before you ticked it.

Rather than pinning the hips to one spot — which also flattens the weight shift and forward lean that make a walk read as a walk — only the low-frequency part of the horizontal motion is removed. The per-step sway survives on top of a stationary root. Vertical motion and all rotations are untouched: jumps still leave the ground, crouches still lower the body, and *"turns left"* still turns, on the spot.

This is also what makes a **looping** clip possible: with the travel left in, the character snaps back to the origin every time the loop restarts, however well the poses match.

## Saved motions

Every generation is saved automatically, as soon as the service returns it — before retargeting, before anything else can go wrong. A generation costs minutes of GPU time; a retarget costs milliseconds, so what is stored is the raw motion, not the version fitted to one mesh.

Click **Select saved motions** to open the picker. It lists everything you have ever generated, **from any project** — a motion describes a body, not a project's content — with its prompt, duration, frame count and date.

| In the picker | |
| --- | --- |
| **Search** | Filters by name and prompt. Selection is kept across searches, so you can search, tick, search again and tick more. |
| **Apply (N)** | Adds the ticked motions to this session's clip grid, exactly where a fresh generation would land. The last one is retargeted and played; the rest bake when you click them. |
| **Delete (N)** | Removes the stored motions — click once to arm it, again to confirm. A clip you have already applied keeps working until you leave the page. |
| **Play** | Applies that one motion immediately. |

Applying a saved motion never touches the GPU: no checkpoint, no text encoder, no diffusion.

## Editing an animation

Generated motion is rarely perfect: a foot sinks on one frame, a shoulder is over-rotated throughout, a walk cycle does not quite loop. Click a clip to play it, then click **Edit animation** to open the **animation editor** — a dock under the viewport that pauses the clip and holds it at one frame.

Opening the editor turns the skeleton overlay on, because most of what you do there starts with clicking a bone on the mesh.

### Getting around

| Control | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Transport** | First / previous / play-pause / next / last frame, a frame number field, and the clip's length, time and frame rate. |
| **Scrub bar** | Drag to any frame. |
| **Animated bones** | The bones this clip drives, in hierarchy order, searchable. Selection is two-way with the mesh and the Skeleton tab: click a bone in the viewport and it is selected here, and the other way round. |

Bones marked with a padlock are driven by the Hand-curl sliders and rebuilt every time the clip is baked, so they cannot be edited here.

### Posing a bone

Select a bone and either type into the **Rotation** and **Position** fields, or drag the gizmo on the mesh.

- **Right-click a bone** to switch its gizmo between **Move** and **Rotate**. The pair of buttons in the editor does the same thing.
- Move drags along the world axes; Rotate turns the bone about its own axes.
- The trash button at the end of each row **clears that value at this frame**: the frame drops its own value and takes the one between its neighbours, which is what deleting the key would look like.
- Rotations are stored as quaternions, so a committed angle is read back from the quaternion — 190° comes back as −170°, and the pose is identical.

Only some bones come out of the bake with a **position** track — usually just the hips, since everything else is rotation-only. Any bone can have one: move it with the gizmo, or click **Add position track**. Every key starts at the bone's rest position, so nothing changes until you edit it.

> Moving a bone rather than rotating it can stretch the skin around it. That is inherent to translating a joint, not a fault — rotate where you can, and move when the pose needs it.

### Apply to

Kimodo's output carries a key on **every** frame, so an edit confined to one frame is a 33 ms pop rather than a correction. **Apply to** decides how far a change reaches:

| Option | Use it for |
| --- | --- |
| **Falloff ±N frames** | The default. The edited frame takes the change in full and the neighbours ease into it. What you want almost always. |
| **This frame** | A deliberate one-frame spike. |
| **Whole clip** | A pose that is wrong all the way through — an over-rotated shoulder, a foot that never quite plants. |

### Frames

The buttons beside the scrub bar change the clip's **length**. The frame rate never changes, so trimming makes the clip shorter, not faster.

| Button | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **+** | Insert a copy of this frame after it, holding the pose one frame longer. |
| **−** | Delete this frame. |
| **Append** | Add a copy of the last frame at the end. |
| **Copy** / **Paste** | Copy the whole frame — every animated bone's rotation plus the hip position — and paste it onto another frame, of this clip or another. With a falloff the neighbouring frames ease into the pasted pose. |
| **«** / **»** | Delete every frame **before** / **after** this one. |
| **∞** | **Smooth transition** — ease the last **±frames** into the start pose so the loop crosses the seam at the clip's own rate. Forward travel is left alone, as is anything already smooth. |

#### Making a clip loop

A generated walk usually ends a dozen frames past the point where the cycle repeats. To close it:

1. Scrub to the frame where the pose comes back round to the start.
2. Click **»** to drop everything after it.
3. Tick **Convert to in-place**, or the character will teleport back to the origin on every loop.
4. Click **∞ Smooth transition** to settle the seam itself.

Engines wrap time when they loop, so the last frame should be the one *just before* the pose repeats — a last frame identical to the first makes the loop stall for a frame, and one that is merely *close* makes it jolt. **Smooth transition** fixes both: it sets the last frame halfway between the second-to-last and the first, which is exactly the step the wrap then produces, so the motion crosses the seam at the same rate as everywhere else.

**How it works, and its one trade-off.** Rewriting only the last frame is not enough: putting it halfway between its neighbour and frame 0 makes the two steps either side of the wrap *equal*, which fixes a clip trimmed near a matching pose but merely splits a big jump into two equal jumps. So the tool eases the last **±frames** into the start pose with a cosine ramp, then sets the last frame halfway between its (blended) neighbour and frame 0 — the loop then crosses the seam at the same rate as the rest of the clip, and the last frame is not a duplicate of the first (which would stall the loop for a frame).

The trade-off is that those last few frames no longer follow the generated motion exactly — they bend towards the start. A bigger **±frames** is smoother and bends more of the clip. Measured on a clip whose legs ended 26° out of phase, against a normal per-frame step of 0.44°: **±8 → 0.42°**, **±24 → 0.04°**.

Two things it deliberately leaves alone: **forward travel** (blending that would slide the character backwards over the tail — it is detected as motion that goes somewhere and stays, rather than motion that oscillates), and anything whose seam step is already no worse than a normal frame step, which is also why clicking twice does nothing.

### Edits, undo and Revert

**Undo** and **Redo** cover everything in the editor, per clip, including frame changes and pasted poses.

An edited clip gets an **edited** badge, in the editor and on its tile in the grid. From that point the clip is **yours**: the bake settings above — Match reference rest pose, Convert to in-place, Hand curl — no longer rebuild it, because rebuilding would throw your edits away. **Revert** hands the clip back to the bake, with whatever those settings say *now*.

Edits belong to the retargeted clip — your mesh's own bones — so they are saved with the mesh, not into the motion library. The stored motion stays clean, ready to be applied to a different character.

## Saving

**Save mesh with N animations** embeds the ticked clips, edits included, and stores the result as a **new version** of the asset. The original is untouched. From there, **[Mesh export](38.md)** carries the rig and its animations to Blender, Unity or Unreal.
